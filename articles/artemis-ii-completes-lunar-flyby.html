Artemis II Astronauts Return Safely After NASA's First Crewed Moon Flyby Since Apollo

A four-person NASA crew splashed down off the California coast in April after completing a roughly 10-day mission that looped around the Moon -- the first time astronauts have traveled beyond low Earth orbit in over 50 years.

By DailyScopePost Editorial Team Published April 11, 2026

Illustration of a spacecraft capsule descending with parachutes. Credit: Original editorial illustration / DailyScopePost.

What happened

NASA's Artemis II mission splashed down safely off the coast of San Diego on April 10, completing a roughly 10-day crewed flight that looped around the Moon and returned to Earth. It marked the first time astronauts have traveled beyond low Earth orbit since NASA's Apollo 17 mission in December 1972 -- more than 50 years earlier.

What we know

The mission launched on NASA's Space Launch System rocket carrying an Orion crew capsule with four astronauts aboard. Unlike Apollo missions, Artemis II was a flyby rather than a landing: the crew traveled around the Moon without touching down, testing Orion's life-support systems, navigation, and heat shield under real deep-space conditions ahead of a planned future landing mission.

NASA has described the mission as a critical proving ground for the hardware and procedures the agency needs before attempting a crewed lunar landing under the Artemis program, its successor to Apollo.

Why it matters

Artemis II is the first crewed test of the exact spacecraft and rocket combination NASA intends to use for an eventual lunar landing mission, Artemis III. A successful flyby validates systems that cannot be fully tested without astronauts aboard, including life support, communications, and the Orion capsule's performance during high-speed reentry into Earth's atmosphere -- one of the most demanding parts of any deep-space mission.

Beyond the technical milestones, the mission represents a significant symbolic moment: it's the first time in over half a century that humans have traveled to the vicinity of the Moon, following decades in which crewed spaceflight was confined to low Earth orbit aboard the Space Shuttle and International Space Station.

Background

The Artemis program was established to return American astronauts to the Moon and eventually support longer-term lunar exploration. Earlier Artemis missions tested hardware without a crew; Artemis II was the program's first flight to carry astronauts. NASA's mission planning for deep-space exploration typically spans years of engineering, testing, and safety reviews before a crewed flight is approved.

Artemis II followed the uncrewed Artemis I mission, which tested the Space Launch System rocket and Orion spacecraft on an uncrewed lunar trajectory. The crewed flyby did not include a lunar landing -- that is planned for a later mission in the program -- but it was designed to validate that Orion's life-support, navigation, and communication systems work as intended with a crew aboard before NASA commits to a landing attempt.

The bigger picture

A successful crewed lunar flyby is widely viewed within the space community as the key technical milestone standing between the Artemis program and an eventual crewed landing, which NASA has targeted for a subsequent mission. The mission also carries symbolic weight as the first time American astronauts have traveled beyond low Earth orbit since the Apollo program ended in the 1970s.

What happens next

NASA has said it will use data from Artemis II to inform preparations for Artemis III, which is intended to include a crewed lunar landing near the Moon's south pole. The precise timeline for that mission depends on the continued development and testing of the landing system and spacesuits required for astronauts to actually set foot on the lunar surface.
