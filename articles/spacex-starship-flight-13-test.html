SpaceX Launches 13th Starship Test Flight, Booster Splashes Down Off Texas Coast

The most powerful rocket ever built lifted off from Starbase, Texas, marking another milestone -- and lingering questions -- in SpaceX's push toward a fully reusable Starship system.

By DailyScopePost Editorial Team Published August 8, 2026

Illustration of a rocket launching with a parachute descent. Credit: Original editorial illustration / DailyScopePost.

What happened

SpaceX launched its Super Heavy-Starship rocket on its 13th test flight on August 8, lifting off from the company's Starbase, Texas launch site at 5:51 p.m. EDT. The Super Heavy booster completed an on-target but "hard" splashdown off the Texas Gulf Coast, while the Starship upper stage carried out a successful suborbital hop, splashing down in the Indian Ocean.

What we know

The 407-foot-tall, two-stage rocket is powered by 33 methane-fueled Raptor engines and generates roughly 16 million pounds of thrust at liftoff -- making it the most powerful rocket ever flown. An earlier launch attempt in July ended in a last-second, computer-commanded abort after several Raptor engines failed to start properly; SpaceX addressed those issues before this flight. The mission followed Starship Flight 12 in May, when an off-angle engine startup sequence and multiple engine failures on the booster prevented a planned boostback burn and resulted in the loss of that booster.

Why it matters

Starship is central to SpaceX's long-term ambitions, including NASA's Artemis program, which has selected a Starship variant as the lunar lander for future crewed Moon landings. Each test flight is designed to validate specific systems -- from engine reliability to booster recovery -- that SpaceX needs to prove out before Starship can be considered ready for operational missions, including the uncrewed and crewed lunar landing flights planned under Artemis.

The lunar lander variant of Starship faces an additional technical hurdle beyond what a standard Earth-orbit mission requires: because the vehicle is too large to launch to the Moon fully fueled, NASA's Artemis architecture depends on SpaceX successfully transferring propellant between Starship vehicles in orbit -- refueling one ship using propellant carried by a series of tanker flights. That capability has not yet been demonstrated in flight, and it remains one of the most closely watched open questions for the Artemis III landing timeline.

What remains unresolved

SpaceX has not disclosed a specific technical explanation for the booster's "hard" splashdown, leaving open whether the issue stemmed from an engine, guidance, or structural problem that will need to be addressed before future flights attempt a controlled catch of the booster rather than an ocean splashdown. NASA has also not said whether the pace of Starship testing to date is fast enough to support the currently planned Artemis III landing window without further schedule adjustments.

Background

SpaceX has flown Starship 13 times since its first test flight, with a mixed record of successes and failures as the company iterates rapidly on the vehicle's design -- a development approach the company has used successfully with its Falcon 9 rocket. NASA has said it remains confident in the Artemis III mission's timeline, though the mission depends on Starship demonstrating capabilities, including in-orbit propellant transfer, that have not yet been fully proven in flight.

What happens next

SpaceX has not announced a specific date for Starship Flight 14, though the next flight is expected to be the first attempt to fly the newer Starship V3 design and could include the program's first attempt to catch the returning Starship vehicle, similar to how SpaceX now routinely catches its Super Heavy boosters. Company officials have said that flight is not expected before September 2026.
