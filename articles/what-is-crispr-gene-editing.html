What Is CRISPR? A Beginner's Guide to Gene Editing

A gene-editing tool adapted from a natural bacterial defense system has become one of the most significant developments in modern biology.

By Dr. Susan Adams Published May 22, 2026 · 4 min read

Illustration of a DNA double helix with a pair of molecular scissors. Credit: DailyScopePost illustration

CRISPR has become one of the most talked-about tools in modern science, associated with everything from new medical treatments to agricultural research. Despite the buzz, the underlying concept is fairly approachable once broken down into its basic parts.

Where CRISPR comes from

CRISPR -- an acronym for "clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats" -- was not invented in a lab from scratch. It was discovered as part of a natural immune system that bacteria use to defend themselves against viruses. When a virus attacks a bacterium, the bacterium can capture a snippet of the virus's genetic material and store it in its own genome as a kind of molecular memory. If that same virus attacks again, the bacterium uses that stored sequence to recognize and destroy the invading virus's DNA.

How scientists adapted it into a gene-editing tool

Researchers realized that the molecular machinery bacteria use to target and cut specific DNA sequences could be reprogrammed to target essentially any sequence of interest, not just viral DNA. The system generally used in the lab, called CRISPR-Cas9, involves two main components:

  • A guide RNA molecule, programmed to match a specific target sequence in a genome.
  • The Cas9 protein, an enzyme that acts like molecular scissors, cutting the DNA at the location the guide RNA points to.

Once the DNA is cut, a cell's own natural repair processes take over, which scientists can leverage either to disable a gene, correct a mutation, or, with additional engineered material provided, insert a new sequence at that exact location.

Why this represented such a significant advance

Earlier gene-editing techniques existed before CRISPR, but they were generally far more difficult, expensive, and time-consuming to redirect toward a new genetic target. CRISPR's guide RNA can be redesigned relatively quickly and cheaply to target a new sequence, which dramatically lowered the barrier to precise gene editing and made the technique accessible to many more research labs. This is a major reason CRISPR spread so quickly through the scientific community after it was adapted for genome editing in the early 2010s.

Real-world applications

  • Medical treatments: CRISPR-based therapies have been developed and approved for certain inherited blood disorders, including sickle cell disease, by editing a patient's own cells to correct or compensate for a disease-causing mutation.
  • Research tools: CRISPR is widely used simply to study gene function, by disabling specific genes in lab organisms to observe what changes as a result.
  • Agriculture: Researchers have used CRISPR to develop crop varieties with traits like improved disease resistance or altered nutritional content.

Limitations and open questions

CRISPR is precise, but not perfect -- it can occasionally cut at unintended locations in the genome, known as off-target effects, which researchers continue working to minimize through improved guide RNA design and enzyme engineering. Editing genes in reproductive cells or early embryos, which would make changes heritable by future generations, raises separate and significant ethical questions that are distinct from editing genes in an individual patient's non-reproductive cells, and this distinction is central to ongoing scientific and regulatory debate about where the technology's boundaries should be.

Why it matters going forward

CRISPR has already moved from a laboratory curiosity to an approved medical treatment for specific conditions in under two decades, and research continues into applying it to a broader range of genetic diseases. Its relatively low cost and flexibility mean it will likely remain a foundational tool in biology and medicine for the foreseeable future, even as newer, more refined gene-editing techniques continue to be developed alongside it.

Beyond Cas9: other CRISPR systems

While Cas9 remains the most widely used and best-studied CRISPR enzyme, researchers have identified and engineered related enzymes with different properties, such as Cas12 and Cas13, some of which can target RNA rather than DNA or offer improved precision for specific applications. This expanding toolkit means CRISPR-based research today is considerably broader than the single original Cas9 system that first drove the technology's rapid adoption.

Key Takeaways

  • CRISPR is adapted from a natural bacterial immune defense system against viruses.
  • The CRISPR-Cas9 system uses a guide RNA to target a specific DNA sequence and an enzyme to cut it.
  • CRISPR-based treatments are already approved for certain inherited diseases, including sickle cell disease.
  • Editing heritable reproductive cells raises distinct ethical questions from editing an individual patient's own cells.

Common Questions

Is CRISPR the same as genetic modification used in older GMOs?

Not exactly. Many older genetically modified organisms were created by inserting genes from a different species using less targeted methods. CRISPR allows much more precise editing of an organism's existing genome, including small, targeted corrections rather than only inserting foreign genes.

Has CRISPR been used to edit human embryos?

A widely condemned case of edited human embryos brought to birth was reported in China in 2018, which violated broad international scientific consensus against heritable human genome editing at that time. Legitimate CRISPR medical treatments approved to date edit a patient's own non-reproductive cells, not embryos.

Is CRISPR gene editing considered safe?

Approved CRISPR-based treatments have gone through standard clinical trial and regulatory review processes in relevant countries, similar to other advanced medical therapies, though as with any new treatment, long-term safety continues to be monitored and researched.
