What Is the European Union? A Guide to How It Works

The European Union links 27 member countries through shared economic and political institutions -- but it isn't a single country, and understanding the difference matters.

By Robert Nguyen Published June 03, 2026 · 4 min read

Illustration of a ring of stars over a stylized map of Europe. Credit: DailyScopePost illustration

The European Union frequently appears in U.S. news coverage of trade, technology regulation, and foreign policy, but its structure -- somewhere between a traditional international alliance and a single federal government -- can be confusing. Here's a grounded overview.

What the EU actually is

The European Union is a political and economic union of 27 European member countries, built through a series of treaties beginning in the years following World War II, initially focused on economic cooperation intended to make future European conflict less likely. Over decades, it expanded from a primarily economic arrangement into a broader union with shared institutions covering trade, regulation, and aspects of foreign and monetary policy, while member countries retain their own governments, and most retain their own national identities, legal systems, and, in most cases, their own currencies outside the eurozone.

Key EU institutions

  • The European Commission proposes new legislation and enforces existing EU law, functioning somewhat like an executive branch for union-wide matters.
  • The European Parliament is directly elected by citizens of member states and shares legislative power with the Council of the European Union.
  • The Council of the European Union represents member state governments and, together with Parliament, must approve most EU legislation.
  • The European Court of Justice interprets EU law and resolves disputes about its application across member states.

The single market and the euro

One of the EU's foundational achievements is the single market, which allows goods, services, capital, and people to move between member countries with far fewer barriers than trade between unrelated nations typically involves. A subset of 20 member countries have also adopted a shared currency, the euro, managed by the European Central Bank, though several EU members -- including relatively large economies -- have chosen to keep their own national currencies.

How much power member countries give up

EU membership involves member states agreeing that EU law takes precedence over conflicting national law in areas covered by EU treaties, and accepting rulings from EU courts in those areas. However, many significant policy areas -- including taxation, healthcare systems, and, notably, foreign and defense policy -- remain primarily under the control of individual national governments rather than centralized EU institutions, which is part of why the EU is often described as neither a simple international organization nor a single federal state, but something distinct from both.

Why the EU matters to the United States

The EU negotiates trade policy as a single bloc on behalf of its member states, making it one of the largest trading partners of the United States. EU regulatory decisions, particularly around technology, data privacy, and competition policy, frequently have effects that extend well beyond Europe, since companies often find it simpler to apply EU-compliant standards globally rather than maintain entirely separate systems for different markets -- a dynamic sometimes referred to as the "Brussels effect."

Brexit and EU membership changes

The United Kingdom left the European Union in 2020 following a 2016 referendum, becoming the first member state to withdraw under the EU's formal exit process. The departure required years of negotiation over trade terms, citizens' rights, and other practical arrangements, illustrating both how deeply integrated EU membership becomes over time and the significant complexity involved in unwinding that integration.

How EU laws are actually implemented

Not all EU rules work the same way inside member countries. Regulations apply directly and uniformly across all member states without needing separate national legislation, while directives set a required outcome but leave it to each country to pass its own laws achieving that outcome, sometimes resulting in variation in the specific rules from one member state to another even though the underlying goal is shared.

For a recent example of EU regulatory reach, see our coverage of the EU's new AI transparency rules, which affect U.S. companies operating there.

Key Takeaways

  • The EU is a union of 27 European countries with shared institutions, not a single country itself.
  • Member states retain significant control over areas like taxation, healthcare, and foreign policy.
  • The EU's single market allows goods, services, capital, and people to move freely between member countries.
  • The EU negotiates trade as a single bloc, making it one of the largest US trading partners.

Common Questions

Do all EU countries use the euro?

No. Twenty of the 27 EU member states use the euro as their currency, forming what's called the eurozone. The remaining members retain their own national currencies, either by choice or because they haven't yet met the economic criteria required to join the eurozone.

Is the EU the same as Europe?

No. Europe is a continent containing many more countries than are EU members, including nations like the United Kingdom, Norway, and Switzerland that are geographically and culturally part of Europe but are not EU members.

Can a country leave the European Union?

Yes. The Lisbon Treaty's Article 50 establishes a formal process for member states to withdraw, which the United Kingdom used to leave the EU in 2020 after a multi-year negotiation process.
