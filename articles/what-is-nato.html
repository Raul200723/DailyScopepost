What Is NATO and Why Does It Matter to the US?

NATO's mutual-defense principle has shaped US foreign policy for more than seven decades. Here's how the alliance actually works.

By Robert Nguyen Published March 26, 2026 Updated August 09, 2026 · 4 min read

Illustration of interlocking flags representing an international alliance. Credit: DailyScopePost illustration

The North Atlantic Treaty Organization, formed in 1949, is a military alliance built around a principle of collective defense among its member countries. Understanding its structure helps explain why it comes up so often in U.S. foreign policy debates.

The core principle: collective defense

Article 5 of the North Atlantic Treaty states that an armed attack against one member is considered an attack against all members, obligating allies to assist -- though the treaty leaves the specific form of assistance to each member's judgment. Article 5 has been formally invoked only once in NATO's history, after the September 11, 2001 attacks on the United States.

How decisions get made

  • NATO operates by consensus, meaning all member states must agree for the alliance to act formally.
  • The North Atlantic Council is the alliance's principal political decision-making body.
  • Member countries maintain their own militaries; NATO coordinates joint planning, exercises, and, when agreed upon, joint operations.

Membership and expansion

NATO began with 12 founding members and has expanded over time, particularly after the end of the Cold War, as additional European countries joined. Membership decisions require unanimous agreement from existing members.

Why it matters to the United States

The U.S. has been a NATO member since its founding and is one of the alliance's largest contributors in terms of defense spending. NATO membership factors into U.S. foreign policy toward Europe, Russia, and broader questions of international security cooperation, and debates over member countries' defense spending levels are a recurring policy topic.

How new members join

Joining NATO requires unanimous agreement from all existing member states, along with the applicant meeting certain political, economic, and military standards outlined in NATO's own accession criteria. This process can take years and has been a significant factor in broader European security debates, particularly regarding countries near Russia's borders.

Defense spending commitments

NATO members have agreed to a guideline of spending at least a set percentage of their GDP on defense, though compliance has varied significantly across member countries over the years. Defense spending levels among allies are a recurring topic in NATO summits and in U.S. foreign policy discussions about burden-sharing within the alliance.

NATO's evolving focus since the Cold War

NATO was founded during the Cold War with a primary focus on collective defense against the Soviet Union. Since the Soviet Union's dissolution, the alliance's stated focus has broadened to include crisis management, partnerships with non-member countries, and responses to newer security challenges such as cyberattacks, while collective territorial defense has regained prominence in recent years amid renewed security concerns in Europe.

How NATO decisions are implemented

Once the North Atlantic Council reaches consensus on a course of action, implementation typically falls to NATO's integrated military command structure, led by a Supreme Allied Commander, working alongside forces and resources voluntarily committed by individual member states for specific operations. This structure allows the alliance to coordinate complex multinational operations while member states retain ultimate control over their own forces.

Article 4 versus Article 5

While Article 5 covers collective defense after an armed attack, NATO's Article 4 allows any member to request formal consultations with the alliance whenever it believes its territorial integrity, political independence, or security is threatened -- without necessarily invoking collective defense. Article 4 consultations have been invoked considerably more often than Article 5, giving member states a formal mechanism to raise security concerns and coordinate a response with allies short of a full collective-defense action.

NATO's civilian and military structure

NATO operates through two parallel structures: a civilian side, headquartered in Brussels and led politically by a Secretary General who chairs the North Atlantic Council, and a military side, which plans and executes operations agreed to by member governments. This division reflects a broader principle running through the alliance -- major decisions, including whether to act militarily at all, remain firmly in the hands of elected national governments rather than NATO's own institutions, which exist to coordinate and implement what members have already agreed to do.

Key Takeaways

  • NATO's Article 5 treats an attack on one member as an attack on all, though the treaty leaves the response to each member's judgment.
  • The alliance operates by consensus, meaning all members must agree for it to act formally.
  • Article 5 has been formally invoked only once, following the September 11, 2001 attacks.
  • The U.S. has been a member since NATO's founding in 1949 and is a major contributor to the alliance.

Common Questions

Is NATO the same as the European Union?

No. NATO is a military alliance with members from North America and Europe, including countries that are not EU members, such as the United States, Canada, and Turkey. The EU is a separate economic and political union of European countries.

Does NATO have its own military?

NATO does not maintain a large standing military of its own separate from member states. Instead, it coordinates joint planning, training, and, when agreed upon, combined operations using forces contributed by member countries.

Can a member country leave NATO?

Yes, the founding treaty allows for withdrawal after providing notice, though no member has done so since NATO's founding in 1949.
