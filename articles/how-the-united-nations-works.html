How the United Nations Works

Founded after World War II, the United Nations was designed to help prevent conflict and coordinate international cooperation. Here's how it's structured.

By Robert Nguyen Published March 20, 2026 Updated August 09, 2026 · 4 min read

Illustration of a globe surrounded by flags. Credit: DailyScopePost illustration

The United Nations was founded in 1945, in the aftermath of World War II, with the stated goal of maintaining international peace and preventing future large-scale conflict. Today it also coordinates work on humanitarian aid, public health, and international law, among other areas.

Main bodies of the UN

  • The General Assembly, where all member states have equal representation and a single vote on most matters.
  • The Security Council, which has primary responsibility for international peace and security and includes five permanent members with veto power: the United States, the United Kingdom, France, Russia, and China, along with ten rotating elected members.
  • The Secretariat, led by the Secretary-General, which carries out the day-to-day work of the organization.
  • Specialized agencies, such as the World Health Organization and UNICEF, which operate somewhat independently under the UN umbrella.

What the UN can and cannot do

The Security Council can authorize measures ranging from sanctions to peacekeeping missions, and in some cases military action, but its permanent members' veto power means it can be blocked from acting when those countries disagree. The General Assembly can pass resolutions, but they are generally non-binding recommendations rather than enforceable law.

How the U.S. is involved

The United States is a founding member and permanent Security Council member, headquarters the UN in New York City, and is historically one of its largest financial contributors.

Common criticisms

Critics across the political spectrum have raised concerns ranging from bureaucratic inefficiency to the Security Council veto power blocking action in major crises. Supporters point to the UN's role in coordinating humanitarian aid, disease response, and diplomatic channels that might not otherwise exist.

How the UN is funded

The UN's regular budget is funded through assessed contributions from member states, calculated using a formula based largely on a country's share of the global economy, along with additional voluntary contributions for specific programs and peacekeeping operations. Because it depends on member state funding, budget disputes among major contributors periodically affect the organization's operations.

Peacekeeping operations

UN peacekeeping missions deploy personnel, often contributed by member states' militaries, to help maintain ceasefires, protect civilians, and support political transitions in conflict-affected regions, subject to Security Council authorization. These missions operate under specific mandates that vary significantly in scope from one deployment to another.

The International Court of Justice

Alongside its political bodies, the UN system includes the International Court of Justice, based in The Hague, which settles legal disputes between states and provides advisory opinions on international legal questions when requested by authorized UN bodies. Its rulings on disputes between states are binding on the parties involved, though the court has no independent enforcement mechanism of its own beyond referring non-compliance to the Security Council.

UN reform debates

Proposals to reform the UN, particularly the Security Council's composition and veto structure, have been discussed for decades, with various countries proposing expanded permanent membership to better reflect current global economic and political realities. Any formal reform would require amending the UN Charter, a process that itself requires approval from a supermajority of member states, including all five permanent Security Council members -- a threshold that has made major structural reform difficult to achieve.

How the UN was founded

The United Nations' founding charter was drafted and signed in San Francisco in 1945, at a conference attended by representatives from 50 countries, building on earlier wartime discussions among Allied powers about how to prevent another global conflict. The organization formally came into existence later that year once the charter was ratified by a required number of signatory countries, including its five prospective permanent Security Council members.

Specialized agencies and their independence

Organizations like the World Health Organization, UNICEF, and the World Food Programme operate under the broader UN umbrella but maintain their own governance structures, funding streams, and leadership, giving them meaningful operational independence from the UN's political bodies. This structure allows technical and humanitarian work to continue even during periods when the Security Council or General Assembly are gridlocked on unrelated political questions.

Key Takeaways

  • The UN's five permanent Security Council members hold veto power over Security Council decisions.
  • General Assembly resolutions are generally non-binding recommendations rather than enforceable law.
  • The U.S. is a founding member, permanent Security Council member, and major financial contributor.
  • Specialized agencies like the WHO and UNICEF operate under the UN umbrella with some independence.

Common Questions

Can the UN force countries to take action?

The Security Council can authorize binding measures, including sanctions and, in some cases, military action, but enforcement still depends on member states' willingness to carry them out, and the veto power of the five permanent members can block Security Council action entirely.

How many countries are UN members?

Nearly all recognized sovereign states are UN members, making it the largest and most broadly representative international organization of its kind.

What is the difference between the UN and NATO?

The UN is a global organization focused broadly on international peace, security, and cooperation across nearly all countries, while NATO is a regional military alliance among a smaller group of member countries built around a mutual-defense commitment.
